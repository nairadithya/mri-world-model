From P0 to lesion forecasting

We first made the comparison trustworthy, then measured how hard the forecasting task really is, and finally taught a model to look directly at lesion anatomy.

The question: given a patient's MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) history, can we predict how the measured lesion burden will change by the next scan? The primary target is three physical measurements in mm³ (cubic millimeters)—enhancing tumor (contrast-bright tumor), nonenhancing/necrotic tumor (tumor tissue that does not brighten with contrast), and edema/FLAIR abnormality (fluid-attenuated inversion recovery: an MRI sequence that highlights fluid-related abnormalities)—rather than a RANO (Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology) category.

LUMIERE is the main training and development cohort. SAILOR is a second-site transfer check; it has helped us find problems, but it is not a fresh, untouched final test. Our simplest yardstick is persistence (predict that each lesion measurement stays at its current value).

P0 — Make the experiment fair

Before judging a model, we needed every method to answer the same question on the same patient pairs. P0 gave each pair a fixed identity, separated past-only forecasts from tasks that can use the follow-up scan, removed eventual survival from prediction inputs, and corrected the metric and patient-level uncertainty code.

We also recovered the LUMIERE lesion masks (voxel maps labeling tumor regions), checked their physical geometry, and aligned the compartment definitions with SAILOR's ONCO masks (its automated tumor segmentation), while recording where mask measurements differ. Volumes are recorded in mm³; errors are scored after a log transform (taking the logarithm, which makes proportional size changes comparable). The primary horizon is “the next observed visit”: dates are not precise enough across both cohorts to claim a fixed number of days.

What P0 gives us: a reproducible, RANO-free anatomy benchmark with explicit exclusions, physical-volume targets, and patient-paired comparisons. P0 is closed for this task.

Still outside that closure: the SAILOR numeric RANO code mapping is unverified, treatment timing is incomplete, and no new untouched external cohort has been reserved. So P0 does not validate clinical response labels or fixed-horizon risk.

P1 — Establish the honest baseline

P1 asked whether the existing JEPA (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture: a model that learns to predict a future scan summary) state predicts next-visit lesion size better than simple physical measurements. We used five-fold patient-separated cross-validation (repeatedly holding out whole patients) on LUMIERE patients not used to train the encoder (network that turns scans into numeric summaries), then applied each fitted model unchanged to SAILOR.

What P1 gives us: a firm negative answer about the old representation: it does not add useful next-visit anatomy forecasts. That ruled out simply scaling up the same JEPA training recipe.

P2 — Build a lesion-aware forecaster

P2 changes what the model sees and how it predicts. The image encoder (network that turns an MRI into numbers) first pools image information inside enhancing, nonenhancing, edema, and nearby-context regions. A transition model (network that learns changes from one visit to the next) then predicts a correction to persistence. The final output remains the three physical lesion measurements.

P2.1–P2.3 · Measure, test, and localize

We built 48 history-only physical features from native masks and scan geometry, including compartment size, shape, prior change, and modality availability. The 48→32 residual MLP (small neural network that adjusts the persistence prediction) tied with persistence on LUMIERE (relative error 0.997) and lost on SAILOR (1.288).

Next we tested the frozen (weights kept fixed) image model more locally. Its whole-brain patch grid often had too little coverage of small tumor compartments, so we added lesion-centred crops (image regions zoomed around the tumor) and separate region tokens (numeric summaries for each region). Those frozen crop and transition features (numbers distilled from image or visit history) still did not beat persistence. They could describe current anatomy in LUMIERE (variance-weighted R², a measure of how much observed variation the decoder explains: 0.691), but this did not transfer to SAILOR (R² −0.155) and did not predict future change.

Intuition: seeing where the lesion is helps describe today's scan, but that alone does not reveal how the lesion will evolve—or make the description consistent across sites.

P2.4 · Train the lesion representation and its transitions

We used recovered LUMIERE masks to teach the encoder what each lesion compartment looks like, then froze that adapted observation model and trained a GRU (gated recurrent unit: a small network that summarizes an ordered sequence) over visit-to-visit changes. A persistence-centred decoder (starts by predicting no change and must learn a useful correction) forecasts the next measurements. The encoder was trained on 65 patients; the locked LUMIERE evaluation patients were kept out of that training.

On eligible locked LUMIERE patients, the learned model's patient-uniform log-volume MAE was 1.183 versus 1.559 for persistence: relative MAE 0.759, with the paired patient-bootstrap 95% confidence interval (range of plausible paired error differences after resampling patients) below zero. Three of the 26 encoder-unseen patients lacked enough recovered masks for this evaluation, so this is a 23-patient result.

Applied unchanged to all 27 SAILOR patients, it scored 0.987 versus persistence at 0.943 (relative MAE 1.047). The uncertainty interval crosses zero, so we cannot say it is better or worse there. A separate support-only calibration (fit a small correction using labeled SAILOR support patients, then score different query patients) reached median relative MAE 0.863 at 20 support patients, but the query set had only seven patients and its confidence interval still crossed zero.

Where we are now: anatomy supervision and a learned transition model solve part of the in-domain problem. Cross-site generalization remains the blocker; support calibration is a lead to test, not a result we can claim as success.

What comes next

  1. Explain the site gap. Check measurement, scanner, preprocessing (image-processing steps), and lesion-state differences that could make learned LUMIERE changes mis-scale on SAILOR.
  2. Bring in independent lesion supervision. Audit available cohorts (including UCSF-ALPTDG, UCSD-PTGBM, and Burdenko) for usable scans, masks, dates, licensing, and overlap before assigning training and test roles.
  3. Test calibration on enough patients. Keep support patients (used to fit the correction) separate from query patients (used only to score it) and report uncertainty; today's seven-query result is too small to settle the question.
  4. Then run a genuinely untouched external test. Keep the model and thresholds fixed before evaluation. Fixed-day forecasts and clinical progression claims also need reliable dates and adjudicated outcomes (clinically reviewed labels).

For now, persistence remains the external bar. More training on the same LUMIERE targets is not justified until the site gap or a new source of supervision is addressed.
